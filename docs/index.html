Claire Whiting

I study how people use language to understand and regulate their emotions, and what that language reveals about mental health from adolescence to adulthood.

I'm a PhD student in Psychology at Princeton University, working with Erik Nook in the Logic of Emotion Lab. My work combines lab, online and field experiments, diary studies, large-scale text analysis and qualitative coding, and increasingly uses large language models in study design and analysis.

I'm interested in research on health, well-being and education.

Linguistic distancing

Describing an experience with fewer first-person, present-tense words, as if seen from further away. My research asks whether this shift helps people regulate emotion or simply accompanies it.

Emotion differentiation

Describing feelings in precise, distinct terms rather than broad ones like “bad” or “stressed”. My systematic review examines how this specificity relates to regulating emotions.

Research

Language and emotion regulation across ages

Four lab, online and field experiments (N = 4,293, including high school students) show that distanced language tracks successful emotion regulation rather than causing it.

Language as a marker of mental health

Using 678,000 messages from roughly 7,000 adolescents on a text-based therapy platform, I examine how psychological distance and language complexity relate to anxiety and depression.

Writing to manage emotions

A 14-day, three-arm diary intervention (N = 159; 2,200+ entries) tested whether writing to oneself or to a close other improves mental health. Benefits differed by condition, and word-embedding measures of self-representation tracked daily emotions. I also led the thematic analysis of these entries.

AI for emotion research

I'm developing an LLM chatbot that guides people through reappraising an upsetting experience, and an LLM-assisted pipeline for qualitative coding of text.

Big team science

I lead a Psychological Science Accelerator study of 13,000+ participants across 25+ countries. I ran the US online pilot (N = 200) and am writing the registered report.

Treatment acceptability

Two experiments (N = 1,010) found that religiosity was associated with the acceptability of Mindfulness-Based Cognitive Therapy. Religious versus scientific framing did not change acceptability; addressing people's concerns did.
